Chaos hits a certain volume and the instinct is always the same: hire someone to
own ops. It feels like progress the day the offer letter goes out. Three months in,
look at what that person's actual week looks like.

## The hire that becomes a manual patch

Too often, the new ops hire's real day-to-day is chasing status updates, retyping
client data between ClickUp and Airtable, and assembling reports from scratch every
cycle - [the same copy-paste work the role was supposed to
fix](/blog/how-much-time-lost-copy-pasting-clickup-airtable). The agency didn't hire
someone to build a system. It hired someone to *be* the system, which means the gap
reopens the moment that person is out sick, on vacation, or gone.

## Why hiring is the instinct even when it's premature

This isn't unique to agencies. [Startup Genome's research across 3,200 high-growth
companies found that 70% showed signs of premature scaling](https://startupgenome.com/insights/premature-scaling-a-deep-dive),
defined in part as spending on things like additional headcount before the
underlying process or stage was actually validated. Hiring feels like the safe,
concrete move when a process is breaking. It's often the expensive one.

## What to fix before the job posting goes up

Split what the role is supposed to cover into two piles: repeatable steps that
follow the same sequence every time (data entry, status updates, report assembly),
and judgment calls that genuinely need a person (client relationship decisions,
prioritization, scope negotiation). Automate the first pile before writing the job
description, then hire for what's left in the second.

## What the hire looks like once the process is fixed

An ops hire who isn't absorbing manual busywork spends their time on the parts that
actually need a person: exceptions, judgment calls, and process design specific to
each client. That's a role someone can grow into, not one they burn out on and leave
within a year, taking all the undocumented process knowledge with them.
