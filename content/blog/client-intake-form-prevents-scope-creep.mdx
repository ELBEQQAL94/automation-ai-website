[Alto Accounting's worked example](https://www.alto-accounting.com/insights/scope-creep-agency-profitability)
puts a number on what scope creep actually costs: a 50,000-pound project quoted at
500 hours and a 30% margin, 15,000 pounds of profit. Add moderate scope creep,
about 25% more hours than quoted, and the same project's margin drops to 12.5%,
profit falls to 6,250 pounds. Nothing about that 25% necessarily felt unreasonable
request by request. The project just ends up costing 58% less profit than it
should have.

## The scope document usually isn't the problem

The common causes of scope creep aren't dramatic: scope documents that never state
what's excluded, small requests that never get logged as a change instead of just
absorbed, no lightweight process for approving an addition before work starts on it.
A scope document with clear exclusions genuinely prevents this. The problem is
where that document's contents come from.

## Where the actual gap opens

A scope document can only exclude what someone thought to ask about. If intake
covers the project name, the budget, and a rough timeline, but not what's explicitly
out of scope or who on the client side actually has approval authority, the
resulting document has nothing to point to when a request shows up that sits
outside it. [The same information gap shows up at the sales-to-delivery
handoff](/blog/sales-to-delivery-handoff-gap-agencies): whatever wasn't captured
early doesn't exist later, when it would actually help.

## What intake needs to ask instead

Two questions do most of the work: what does success not include, stated as
explicitly as what it does, and who besides this person has to sign off before
something is approved. The second question matters as much as the first, since
[a scope conversation that only reflects one stakeholder's understanding tends to
surface a second, disagreeing one later](/blog/multi-stakeholder-approvals-three-people-sign-off),
usually after work has already started against the first version.
