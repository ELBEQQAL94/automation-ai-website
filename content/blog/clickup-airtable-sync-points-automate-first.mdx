[Neither tool syncs the other natively](https://unito.io/blog/clickup-airtable-integration/).
Airtable's own embed feature puts a view of Airtable data inside ClickUp, but it
only works one direction, it's read-only, and it won't even show a change until
someone refreshes the page. Real two-way sync, where an update in either tool shows
up in the other, only happens through a third-party layer: Zapier, Make, Unito, or a
custom-built connection. That layer has to be built deliberately, which means
someone has to decide what it actually covers first.

## Not every sync point deserves the same priority

Building one automation that tries to sync everything between the two tools at once
usually creates something brittle that breaks in ways nobody notices until a client
does. The sync points worth building first are the ones that are both high
frequency and visible when they drift out of sync, not the ones that are merely
possible to connect.

## Where to actually start

New client setup comes first: when a record gets created in Airtable, the matching
ClickUp project should exist without anyone re-typing it, since [this is the same
gap that costs agencies the most at the start of a relationship](/blog/sales-to-delivery-handoff-gap-agencies).
Status changes come second: a task's status in ClickUp is the thing that drifts out
of Airtable fastest, because it changes daily and nobody remembers to update a
second system every time. Reporting numbers come third, lower frequency but highest
cost when wrong, since [that's the data a client actually reads](/blog/campaign-reporting-cadence-what-to-automate-vs-what-clients-read).

## What to leave manual, at least at first

Anything that changes rarely and stays internal, a client's billing contact, an
account note, a tag used for internal categorization, can stay a manual update
longer without costing much. [Signs a sync is about to break](/blog/signs-your-clickup-airtable-sync-is-about-to-break)
tend to show up first in the high-frequency points anyway, so that's also where
fixing it first pays off fastest.

## Where this fits into the bigger picture

This is the same sequencing logic behind [what's actually safe to automate versus
what needs a human](/blog/simple-test-what-to-automate-vs-what-needs-a-human):
repeatable and highly visible when wrong gets automated first, everything else can
wait. [See how workflow design builds that sync layer around the points that
actually matter](/services), instead of one automation trying to cover everything
at once.
