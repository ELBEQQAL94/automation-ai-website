Most automation frameworks score a task on two axes: how repeatable it is, and how
expensive a mistake in it would be. High repeat, low mistake cost, automate it.
Low repeat, high mistake cost, leave it to a person. [RedHub AI's back-office
framework](https://blog.redhub.ai/back-office-tasks-to-automate) sums up the rule in
one line: automate the repetition, keep the judgment.

## Where the test needs a stricter rule for agencies

That two-axis test works fine for internal admin work, but it breaks down the moment
a task's output is something a client actually reads. A weekly status email is
repeatable and looks low-risk by the framework's own logic, right up until it goes
out with the wrong client's name in it or a number that doesn't match what was
actually delivered. The mistake is cheap to produce and expensive to have happened.
For agency work, the fix is to stop scoring "cost of a mistake" on the task itself
and score it on who sees the result: if a client's eyes land on the output, the cost
defaults to high no matter how routine the underlying steps are. That's the same
logic behind [why agencies shouldn't fully automate client-facing
work](/blog/why-agencies-shouldnt-fully-automate-client-facing-work) and [what human-in-the-loop
automation actually means](/blog/human-in-the-loop-automation-explained) in practice:
automate what builds the report, keep a person on what sends it.

## The harder problem is finding the task at all

[Summit Trails' Wendy Kinney](https://summittrails.com/blog/how-to-know-what-to-automate-in-operations/)
points at the actual bottleneck in this process: "the criteria are easy and widely
known. The hard part is locating those tasks, because they are blended into roles and
hidden from org charts." An account manager's job title doesn't tell you they spend
four hours a week retyping the same client fields into two different tools. That work
only shows up when someone tracks what a role actually does hour to hour, not what
its title implies.

## Applying it to agency ops specifically

Run the test in two passes. First, list what a given role actually does in a normal
week, not what the job description says. Second, for each item, ask whether a client
ever sees the direct output. If yes, that step stays human no matter how repeatable
it is, but the steps that feed into it (pulling status from ClickUp, pulling numbers
from Airtable, formatting the draft) are exactly the kind of [copy-paste work worth
automating first](/blog/how-much-time-lost-copy-pasting-clickup-airtable). If no,
score it on repeatability and mistake cost like any other back-office task.

## What this changes in practice

This is the same principle behind [Automatoro's framework for automating the boring
80% of agency ops](/blog/automating-the-boring-80-percent-agency-ops-framework): the
automatable 80% is everything that happens before someone is ready to hit send. The
client-facing moment itself was never part of that 80%. Run a role through this
two-pass test before deciding it's "too client-facing to touch," and [see how
workflow design draws that line in practice](/services).
