Software teams call it the bus factor: how many people would need to disappear
before the project stalls. A bus factor of one means a single person holds
knowledge or access nobody else has, which makes a resignation and a two-week
vacation cost the business the exact same thing, minus the severance.

## This is already the default state for most agencies

[71% of small businesses report depending on one or two key individuals for
organizational success](https://www.partnermd.com/blog/what-is-key-person-risk).
That's not a rare failure mode, it's closer to the norm, which means most agencies
already have a bus factor of one somewhere in the org chart without having named it
as a risk. It usually isn't the owner. It's whoever became the person everyone
routes a specific kind of question to.

## What it costs once the dependency turns real

Replacing a key employee can cost up to 200% of their annual salary once the
dependency isn't hypothetical anymore. [Mercer Marsh's research puts a timeline on
it](https://www.partnermd.com/blog/what-is-key-person-risk): 67% of businesses
surveyed expect key person risk to affect them within three years, and 55% rate the
potential impact as high. A vacation is the cheap version of this same risk showing
up early, before it's a resignation, while it's still fixable for the cost of a few
uncomfortable days instead of a six-figure scramble.

## The test that actually reveals it

Ask, honestly, whether someone else could run this person's week without a handoff
call. If the answer is no, the bus factor is already one, and the person's next
vacation is the next time it gets tested. [The same problem showing up as
redundancy instead of a true gap](/blog/two-people-one-job-not-coverage) is
different from this: two people sharing ambiguous ownership at least have someone
present. A true single point of failure has nobody at all once that one person is
out.

## Why cross-training alone doesn't close it

The usual fix is having someone shadow before the vacation, a quick knowledge
transfer the week before. That helps once, for that trip, and evaporates by the next
one. [The same gap already covered for new hires](/blog/agencies-re-explain-onboarding-info-new-hires)
applies here too: the fix isn't a one-time handoff conversation, it's whether the
process that person runs lives somewhere a second person could actually pick it up
cold, not just after a briefing right before the flight.
