Focus Digital's Chase McGee names a specific, high-impact churn driver that has
nothing to do with the agency's own work: ["when a client's marketing director
departs, the agency relationship often fails to survive the transition to new
leadership"](https://focus-digital.co/average-marketing-agency-churn/). Nobody
missed a deadline. Nothing was under-delivered. The account still ends, because the
person who understood why the engagement existed left the building.

## The size bracket where this actually matters most

[McGee's churn data by agency size](https://focus-digital.co/average-marketing-agency-churn/)
puts 11-to-25-employee agencies at 24% annual churn and 26-to-50-employee agencies
at 19%, both meaningfully better than sub-10-employee agencies at 32%. Growing past
that early-stage churn rate doesn't make stakeholder turnover stop happening, it
just means more of an agency's remaining churn risk comes from exactly this kind of
loss rather than from the agency's own mistakes.

## What the departing contact actually took with them

The new contact didn't inherit a bad relationship, they inherited none of one. The
original scope conversation, the goals that got agreed to before a contract existed,
the reason a particular approach was chosen over another, all of that lived in a
relationship between two people, and only one of them is still around. Whatever
wasn't written into the project record left when they did.

## The same gap, later in the relationship

This is the same failure mode as [the sales-to-delivery handoff
gap](/blog/sales-to-delivery-handoff-gap-agencies), just happening months into an
engagement instead of at the start of one. In both cases, the information that
matters lived in a person's head or inbox instead of a record the next person could
actually open. [When no one owns the whole client account](/blog/when-no-one-owns-the-whole-client-account)
either, that gap gets worse: three specialists can each explain their own slice to a
new contact, but nobody hands over the whole picture at once.

## What changes the outcome

None of this means automating the relationship away. A new stakeholder still needs
an actual person walking them through where things stand and why. What changes the
outcome is whether that person can pull the real history in minutes; the original
scope, the goals, the decisions already made, instead of reconstructing it from
memory and old email while the new contact is deciding whether this agency still
makes sense. [The same record that should carry a client through onboarding](/blog/onboarding-new-clients-without-the-spreadsheet-chaos)
is what carries them through a stakeholder change too, since it's the same
information, just needed again later.

## Where this fits into the bigger picture

This is the practical version of [automating the boring 80% of agency
ops](/blog/automating-the-boring-80-percent-agency-ops-framework): keep a system of
record accurate enough that losing one person on the client side doesn't mean
starting over. [See how workflow design keeps that record current](/services)
before the next contact change puts it to the test.
