The first automated workflow almost always works. Someone automates status reporting
for one client, it holds up fine for a few weeks, and everyone agrees this should
happen everywhere. Six months later, it's often still running for just that one
client. Nothing broke. It just never left the pilot.

## Why the pilot working isn't the same as being ready

[AI Assembly Lines lists the specific mistakes mid-market companies make here](https://aiassemblylines.com/post/ai-pilots-fail-scale-5-mistakes-mid-market-companies-make),
and two translate directly to agency work. First, pilots run on curated,
hand-picked conditions that don't match everything else: "85% of all AI projects
fail due to poor data quality," and a pilot built on one client's clean, simple
account structure hasn't been tested against the messier ones. Second, the people
who'd actually run the thing day to day usually weren't part of building it: "70% of
change programs fail due to employee resistance and lack of management support,"
which in an agency looks like account managers who were never shown how the
automation works and have no reason to trust output they didn't build.

## The ownership gap nobody assigns

The same source cites a third factor: "only 21% of organizations have a mature
governance model" once a pilot concludes, meaning nobody owns deciding what happens
next. For an agency, that's the automation staying in the personal workflow of
whoever built it, instead of becoming something the team runs. [The same gap shows up whenever
no one owns the whole client account](/blog/when-no-one-owns-the-whole-client-account):
a system with no assigned owner doesn't expand on its own, regardless of how well it
worked the first time.

## What actually proves a pilot is ready

The real test isn't whether the pilot worked, it's whether someone besides the
person who built it can run it on a different client without that person in the
room. That's a higher bar than "it worked once," and it's the same bar [human-in-the-loop
automation](/blog/human-in-the-loop-automation-explained) depends on: if a person
still has to review the output before it reaches a client, that review needs to hold
up against a messier account too, not just the clean one the pilot launched on.

## Applying it to the next rollout

Before calling a pilot done, hand it to someone who didn't build it and point it at
the least tidy client on the roster. [The same two-question test for what's safe to
automate](/blog/simple-test-what-to-automate-vs-what-needs-a-human) applies here:
if the pilot only holds up under ideal conditions, it isn't finished, it's just
working once. [See how workflow design gets a pilot ready for every client it needs
to run on](/services), not just the first one.
