A client relationship ends and there's rarely a plan for it. Onboarding gets a
checklist, a project template, sometimes a whole playbook. Offboarding gets whoever
remembers to revoke Slack access before it slips their mind.

## Why offboarding never gets built

Onboarding has a built-in trigger: a deal closes, revenue is on the line, and someone
makes sure the checklist runs. Offboarding has no equivalent moment. [Nobody schedules
a checklist](/blog/onboarding-new-clients-without-the-spreadsheet-chaos) for work
that's ending instead of starting, so it defaults to memory, and memory is exactly
what breaks first once the account stops being top of mind.

## The access nobody remembers to revoke

The gap shows up first as a security problem, not a process one. Even in employee
offboarding, where companies have far more incentive to get this right, [38% of
employees report accessing a prior employer's accounts after
leaving](https://1password.com/blog/the-hidden-offboarding-step-draining-your-budget).
Client offboarding runs on weaker discipline than that: shared drives, dashboards, and
platform seats granted over the life of an account rarely get revoked as a matter of
routine when the account closes.

## The exit that's actually a retention opportunity

Treating offboarding as a formality also means missing it as a save opportunity. One
customer success platform found that [a structured exit process reversed 25% of
cancellation requests](https://www.custify.com/blog/referrizer-saved-clients-through-offboarding/),
worth roughly $56,000 in two months. A cancellation request isn't automatically a
closed door if there's a real conversation instead of a silent exit.

## What a repeatable offboarding actually looks like

The fix is the same shape as fixing onboarding: stop relying on someone to remember,
and make the process self-tracking. A project status change to "closed" should
trigger the checklist on its own: revoke platform access, archive shared files,
schedule the exit conversation, log the reason the client left - instead of hoping
whoever's closest to the account handles it manually.
