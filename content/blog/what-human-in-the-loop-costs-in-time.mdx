Ask why an agency hesitates to automate a client-facing step and "we want a human to
check it first" comes up fast. The unspoken worry right behind that is what the
check itself costs: does keeping a person in the loop just rebuild the bottleneck
automation was supposed to remove?

## The actual target is under two minutes

[AI Solutions for Small Biz puts a number on it](https://aisolutionsforsmallbiz.com/resources/how-to-add-human-review-to-ai-workflows/):
"a review gate that takes 10 minutes is not sustainable. A good review interface
should take under 2 minutes for most items." Their diagnosis of what makes the
difference matters more than the number itself: "if review is taking too long, look
at the interface and the prompts, not at the human reviewer." A slow review usually
isn't a slow person, it's a review step that never got designed.

## Why the automation shouldn't wait on the review either

[Marco Coronado's framing of the checkpoint itself](https://semnexus.com/workflow-automation-handoff-points-where-human-review-belongs)
adds the other half: a well-built checkpoint is asynchronous, not blocking. "The
automation doesn't halt, it routes the flagged item to a queue and continues
processing everything else." The two-minute review cost applies to that one item,
on the reviewer's own schedule, not to the whole pipeline sitting idle until someone
opens their inbox.

## What actually makes a review slow

Coronado names the real driver: "a reviewer who has to reconstruct context from
scratch will make slower, worse decisions." That's the same problem this site keeps
circling back to from different angles, [a new client contact inheriting no
context](/blog/client-point-of-contact-changes-mid-project), [a pilot that only
holds up under ideal conditions](/blog/why-automation-projects-stall-after-the-pilot).
A fast review depends on everything the reviewer needs already sitting in front of
them: the original input, what changed, why it's flagged, not a search through three
tools to reconstruct what's actually being approved.

## What this changes about the tradeoff

[Human-in-the-loop automation](/blog/human-in-the-loop-automation-explained) isn't
a fixed cost that gets paid every time a person stays involved. It's a design choice
with a real, controllable price, and that price is usually close to zero once the
review step is built around the two things above: a pre-assembled draft and a queue
that doesn't block anything else. [See how workflow design builds that review step
in](/services), instead of treating "keep a human in the loop" as the same thing as
"slow it down."
