CPA Igor Tutelman lays out the diagnostic in one line: ["an agency that grows from
$2M to $3M in revenue while headcount grows from 12 to 20 people has become less
productive per person, regardless of how the top line looks."](https://iota-finance.com/iota-finance-blog/revenue-per-employee)
Revenue went up 50%, headcount went up 67%. That's growth on paper. On the ratio
that actually predicts trouble, this agency is worse off than before it hired.

## The line that separates functional from structural

Tutelman's benchmarks give the ratio actual meaning: below $120,000 in revenue per
employee "typically signals a structural problem, overstaffing, significant
underpricing, or too much non-billable drag." $120K to $160K is functional but
compressed, with little cash cushion. $160K to $220K is where well-run agencies
tend to sit. His worked example shows how fast the line moves: a $2.2M agency at 12
FTEs sits at $183K per employee, comfortably healthy. The same agency at 15 FTEs
drops to $147K, a materially different, more fragile position, without a single
dollar of revenue changing.

## Where a 20-to-50-person agency actually lands

Run the math for that range specifically, 20 to 50 employees, $3M to $12M in
revenue: the small end lands at $150,000 per employee, right in the
compressed band. The large end lands at $240,000, into the range Tutelman flags as
strong pricing power. That's a wide spread for the same rough headcount bracket, and
most agencies in this range are closer to the compressed end than they'd guess,
which means a bad quarter of hiring is enough to cross into the structural-problem
zone, not just a bad quarter of sales.

## The ratio hides two different problems

A low number doesn't always mean too many people. It sometimes means the right
number of people spending too much of their week on work that isn't billable:
status chasing, retyping client data, assembling reports by hand. [The hiring trap](/blog/the-hiring-trap-why-headcount-wont-fix-a-broken-process)
covers the first case, where the fix is fewer people. This ratio also catches the
second case, where the team is the right size and the fix is freeing up the hours
already being paid for, [the same math behind how many clients one account manager
can actually carry](/blog/how-many-clients-can-one-account-manager-handle) before
admin work eats the capacity a client relationship needs.

## Reading the number correctly

Before treating a slipping ratio as a hiring-freeze signal, check what the added
headcount is actually doing with their time. If it's billable client work, the
ratio will recover as those people ramp up. If it's [absorbing the same manual
busywork a system should be doing](/blog/automating-the-boring-80-percent-agency-ops-framework),
more headcount won't fix the ratio, it'll just repeat it at a larger scale. [See how
workflow design recovers billable capacity](/services) without adding a line to
payroll.
