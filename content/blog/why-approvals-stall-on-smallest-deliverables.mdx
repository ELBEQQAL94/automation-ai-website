C. Northcote Parkinson watched a committee approve an entire nuclear power plant
with barely a comment, then spend a disproportionate share of the meeting arguing
about the materials for a bicycle shed. The plant was the actual decision, the shed
was trivial, and the shed got the debate. [Parkinson's law of triviality names the
reason](https://www.techtarget.com/whatis/definition/Parkinsons-law-of-triviality-bikeshedding):
people focus on smaller details because they're easier to understand than the
complex ones, so everyone in the room has an opinion worth voicing.

## Why this maps directly onto agency approvals

A full campaign strategy usually clears fast, because most people on the client
side trust the agency's expertise on strategy and don't feel qualified to argue
with it. A one-line social caption or a button color gets three rounds of
conflicting notes, because everyone has a genuine opinion about wording and color
in a way they don't about media mix or audience targeting. The deliverable that
matters least to the outcome is the one every stakeholder feels entitled to weigh
in on.

## Where the approval process usually protects the wrong thing

Most agencies build review rigor around the big deliverable: extra sign-off steps,
multiple internal passes, a formal presentation for the strategy doc. The small
stuff gets sent informally, "just approve this real quick," on the assumption it
won't need much. That's backwards. [Bikeshedding means the small item is exactly
where the process needs structure](/blog/multi-stakeholder-approvals-three-people-sign-off):
one point of contact and a defined feedback window, not an open thread where every
stakeholder gets an equal, unbounded say on the font.

## What that structure actually looks like

The fix isn't fewer opinions, people are allowed to have one. It's a defined window
and a single point of consolidation, [the same fix already covered for scattered
client feedback](/blog/what-to-do-when-clients-hold-up-approval): one thread, one
deadline, one person reconciling the notes, so three stakeholders debating a
caption doesn't quietly cost the same turnaround time as the strategy review it was
supposed to be faster than.

## Where this fits into approvals overall

This is the specific mechanism behind [why approval bottlenecks cost agencies
billable hours](/blog/why-client-approval-bottlenecks-cost-agencies-billable-hours):
the hours don't disappear on the big, important calls, they disappear on the small
ones that felt too minor to need a process. [See how workflow design puts structure
around the deliverables that actually stall](/services), not just the ones that
look like they should.
